Notice changes between infusions

Some chemotherapy drugs can affect the nerves in your feet and hands, which can change how you walk, balance, and use your hands. Before each infusion, take a two-minute check: answer a few questions, walk for 30 seconds, stand still, and do a quick tap test. Your twin shows how things are changing over your treatment, and the report gives your care team the details.

Your function twin

Each test on its own is noisy: a tired day or different shoes can change one result. Your twin combines all your check-in signals, weighs each by how reliable it is, and estimates how much your walking, balance, and hand function have changed since your first check-in, with an honest range. It measures function, not your nerves directly. Only your care team can tell whether a change is from nerve damage or something else, like fatigue.

How the twin works

The twin is a state-space model (a Kalman filter), the same kind of model engineers use to track things they can't see directly. It assumes each chemo cycle may bring a little change. At each check-in it compares that expectation with your signals (feet and hand symptoms, walking pace, step timing, step evenness, standing sway, eyes-closed balance, finger tapping, and trouble with small objects), trusting the less noisy signals more.

When a check-in is much worse than the twin expected, it's flagged. That's the kind of change worth telling your care team about before your next infusion.

The model's settings are reasonable starting values for this prototype, not clinically calibrated. It doesn't diagnose, doesn't measure your nerves directly, and doesn't predict your future. Changes like these can come from nerve damage, but also from fatigue, anemia, pain, or other causes, which is why they're worth showing your care team.

Walk for 30 seconds

  • Find a hallway or room where you can walk safely. Use your cane or walker if you normally do.
  • Put your phone in a front trouser pocket, screen facing in.
  • Walk at your usual pace. Turn around when you need to.
  • Your phone will say "start walking" and "stop" out loud.
30

Tap start, then put your phone in your pocket. You'll have 5 seconds.

Stand still for 20 seconds

  • Stand right next to a wall, counter, or sturdy chair you can grab.
  • Keep the phone in the same front pocket.
  • Put your feet side by side, close together, eyes open. Look straight ahead.
  • If you feel unsteady at any point, hold on. Safety first. You can skip this test.
20

Tap start, put the phone in your pocket, and stand with feet together.

Optional: the same test with your eyes closed

Doctors use this test (called the Romberg test) because some nerve problems in the feet show up as extra wobble when you can't see. Your twin compares it with your eyes-open result.

Only do this with a wall or counter right beside you and someone nearby if possible. Keep one hand ready to hold on. If you feel unsteady, open your eyes and hold on. Skip it if your care team has told you to avoid this, or if you've fallen recently.
20

Your phone will say "close your eyes" and "open your eyes".

Tap test for your hands

Use your right index finger. Tap the two circles back and forth, one then the other, as fast as you comfortably can. The 10 seconds start with your first tap.
